Pacifier Use and Colic

Pacifiers are widely recommended for infant sleep safety. The evidence for pacifiers reducing colic specifically is weaker than most parents are told.

4 minute read · Diagnosis & Trust

Good evidence for sleep safety. Thin, and possibly reversed, evidence for colic specifically.

This one deserves a more honest answer than most colic content gives it, because the popular claim doesn't fully hold up.

Non-nutritive sucking, sucking without feeding, has real, separate evidence behind it for two things: supporting self-soothing generally, and reducing the risk of sudden infant death syndrome, a finding solid enough that the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends pacifier use at sleep time for exactly that reason.

What the evidence does not clearly show is that pacifiers reduce colic specifically. A population study of more than 10,000 infants, cited in a widely referenced American Family Physician review of pacifier risks and benefits, found that pacifier users actually had a higher rate of colic and earache compared to infants who didn't use one, not a lower rate. That doesn't mean pacifiers cause colic; the study design can't establish that direction of cause and effect, and babies with harder temperaments may simply be offered pacifiers more often in the first place. But it does mean the common assumption, "give her a pacifier, it'll help the colic," isn't something the research actually backs up.

So where does that leave a pacifier, practically? It's a reasonable, well-supported tool for general self-soothing and sleep safety, on its own merits. It's just not a colic fix, and treating it as one risks missing that the crying is coming from an actual root cause, gut, nervous system, or feeding mechanics, that a pacifier was never going to address. It sits alongside gripe water and gas drops in that respect, more common than effective for colic specifically; see Colic Myths vs. Facts for the other two.
