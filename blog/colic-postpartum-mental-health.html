Colic and Postpartum Mental Health

Colic and maternal mental health are genuinely connected, per real research, not just an assumption. Here's what the evidence shows and what to do with it.

5 minute read · Journey & Support

A prospective study of over 1,000 mothers found the toll can outlast the crying itself.

It's a strange, isolating thing to sit with: you know, rationally, that colic resolves. You've read it, you've been told it, some part of you believes it. And it still doesn't touch the exhaustion or the creeping sense that something in you is breaking down under it. That gap between knowing and feeling is real, and it's worth naming directly rather than glossing over with reassurance alone.

There's real research behind the connection, not just an assumption. Vik and colleagues published a prospective study in Acta Paediatrica (2009) following 1,015 mothers and found that infant crying was associated with elevated maternal depression scores at both two and six months, and notably, colic at two months predicted higher depression scores four months later, even in cases where the crying itself had already resolved by then. That second finding matters: it suggests the toll of an intense early stretch can outlast the crying itself, which is worth knowing rather than assuming everything resets the moment the baby settles.

It's worth being precise about what this research does and doesn't establish. It shows an association, not a guarantee that colic will affect every parent this way, and not proof that colic alone causes depression in a direct, one-to-one sense. Many things intersect with early postpartum mental health. But an association this consistently observed, in a study of over a thousand mothers, is a real signal that the toll colic takes isn't only physical, and it's not something to dismiss as simply being tired.

Worth acting on, not just reading about: talk to your doctor or midwife if low mood, persistent anxiety, or a sense of disconnection is sticking around, especially past the point where the crying itself has eased. That gap between "the baby is better" and "I don't feel better" is exactly the pattern this research flags, and it deserves a real conversation, not just more time.

This site's diagnostic framework addresses the baby's crying pattern specifically. It isn't built to evaluate a parent's mental health, and nothing here should stand in for that conversation with a professional. If you're further along and want the concrete side of what's likely driving the crying itself, the root-cause guide is the place to start.

If you're experiencing thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, or feel unable to cope, contact your doctor, a crisis line, or emergency services right away. You don't have to carry this alone.
