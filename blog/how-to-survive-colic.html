How to Survive Colic Without Losing Your Mind

July 31, 20267 min readBy Vincent, Epidemiologist

Nobody tells you what the sleep deprivation actually does to you until you're living inside it. It isn't just tiredness. It affects judgment, patience, memory, the ability to finish a sentence you started thirty seconds ago. Add to that an elevated stress hormone response that doesn't get much chance to come back down, real strain on the relationship with your partner, handoffs instead of conversations, and a quiet, corrosive sense that you must be doing something wrong, because everyone else seems to be managing and you are barely holding on. None of that is weakness. It's what this specific season does to almost anyone going through it, and naming it plainly is worth more than being told to hang in there.

The part nobody tells you

Here is the piece that usually gets left out entirely. Your own stress state is not separate from your baby's experience. It transmits, physically, through touch, through the tension in your arms and shoulders when you're holding a crying baby for the fortieth minute, through your tone even when you're trying to sound calm. A parent in a heightened stress state, holding a baby who is also dysregulated, is not adding a calming influence to the situation, however much they want to be. This is not a guilt-inducing fact. It's the opposite. It means your own regulation is part of the actual protocol, not a nice-to-have on top of it, which means taking care of your own state isn't indulgent, it's mechanically relevant to what's happening with your baby.

What genuinely helps

Not the generic advice to take a break, which usually isn't available anyway at eleven at night with a screaming infant and a partner who's also exhausted. What actually helps is smaller and more specific than that. A single, deliberate breath cycle, four seconds in, six seconds out, held for even thirty seconds before picking your baby back up, measurably shifts your own physiological state before you re-enter the interaction. Handing off for even ten minutes, not because the other person will magically succeed where you didn't, but because a fresh nervous system in the room changes the dynamic more than another attempt from an already-depleted one. And having an actual sequence to follow, rather than improvising in the moment, removes a layer of decision fatigue that is quietly exhausting in a way most parents don't notice until it's gone.

What to tell your partner

The conversation that tends to help most isn't an emotional check-in, as valuable as those are separately. It's a mechanical handoff plan, agreed on ahead of time, not negotiated in the moment when everyone is already at their limit. Decide in advance who takes the first two hours of the evening window and who takes the second, so neither of you is guessing or resenting the other for not offering first. Decide what the signal is for "I need to tap out right now," a specific word or gesture, so it doesn't have to be explained mid-crisis. The goal isn't dividing the suffering evenly. It's removing the negotiation from the hardest moments, so the actual hard moments can be about your baby instead of about the two of you figuring out logistics under pressure.

The decision tree for tonight, when nothing is working

If you've been at it for twenty minutes and nothing has helped even briefly, the most useful move usually isn't trying harder at the same thing. It's switching systems entirely, moving from a gut-focused attempt to a nervous-system one, or vice versa, since continuing to push on an approach that clearly isn't landing tends to raise everyone's stress further without changing the outcome. And if you notice your own state sliding past frustration into something that feels genuinely unsafe, for you or for how you might handle the next few minutes, putting your baby down safely and stepping into another room for sixty seconds is not a failure. It's the single most protective thing available to you in that moment, for both of you.
