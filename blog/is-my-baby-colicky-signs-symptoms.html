Is My Baby Colicky? Signs, Symptoms, and When It's Something Else

Colic has a real clinical definition, not just "cries a lot." Here's how to tell colic apart from reflux, hunger, and normal newborn fussiness.

5 minute read · Diagnosis & Trust

Every newborn cries. The question is whether this fits a specific, named pattern, or something else entirely.

Every newborn cries. What you're actually trying to figure out at 11pm, holding a baby who won't settle, is whether this is ordinary newborn fussiness or something with an actual name and an actual cause.

The clinical definition traces back to a 1954 study by pediatrician Morris Wessel and colleagues (published in Pediatrics), who followed 98 infants and defined colic by what's since become known as the "rule of threes": crying for more than three hours a day, more than three days a week, for more than three weeks. We cover where that definition holds up and where it's been revised in The 3-3-3 Rule for Colic.

What points toward colic specifically:

  • Crying clusters in the evening, often the same window every day
  • Baby's legs pull up toward the stomach, back may arch
  • Crying is hard to interrupt with the usual soothing that works at other times of day
  • Baby is otherwise feeding, gaining weight, and developing normally between episodes

What points toward something else: Reflux crying tends to worsen during or right after feeds, not hours later, and often comes with spit-up, arching during feeding, or refusal to feed. We wrote a full comparison in Colic vs. Reflux if that's the pattern you're seeing.

The reassuring part first: colic is common, it's not a sign you're doing something wrong, and Savino et al.'s 2010 randomized controlled trial (Pediatrics) found meaningful crying reduction within three weeks using a targeted gut-focused intervention in breastfed infants, one of several approaches with real evidence behind it. See the full root-cause breakdown for what applies to your baby's feeding method. If the question underneath all of this is whether colic leaves any lasting mark, that has a real, specific answer too; see Colic and Long-Term Development.

The honest part second: "colic" isn't one diagnosis with one cause. It's a pattern of crying that typically comes from one of three underlying systems, gut, nervous system, or feeding mechanics, and treating it well starts with knowing which one you're dealing with, not guessing.
